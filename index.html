I’m a master’s student in aerospace engineering at Washington University in St. Louis, working across aerodynamics, structures and payload systems for WashU Design, Build, Fly, our AIAA Design/Build/Fly team. PRJ — DBF 2026–27 SENSOR & CONTAINER

Towed sensor & shipping container: DBF 2026–27

Chief Engineer — Fall 2026, in progress
+

Problem & Constraints

The 2026–27 AIAA DBF mission is a surveillance UAV. The aircraft has to carry a sensor inside a protective shipping container, then deploy, tow, operate, and recover that sensor in flight on a tow line at least 1.5× the wingspan. On the ground mission, the container has to survive repeated drops from a test rig. Sensor weight multiplies directly into the score, so the sensor has to be heavy and still fly stably at the end of a cable, and the container has to protect it while adding as little weight as possible.

My Exact Role

As Chief Engineer, I lead the technical design of the sensor and shipping container: running the fin-configuration trade, the sensor CFD, and the container design, and making the final configuration calls for the team’s proposal.

Technical Execution

  • 3D-printed sensor prototypes and wind-tunnel tested four fin arrangements: cruciform, three-fin-up, three-fin-down, and dihedral
  • Ran SolidWorks Flow Simulation on the sensor body to check drag, lift, and the yaw, pitch, and roll moments it puts on the tow line
  • Specified a 6061 aluminum sensor body with cruciform aluminum fins, a 2S LiPo, and an A-Star 32U4 Micro controller driving the mission’s light modes
  • Designed the shipping container: a machined 6061 aluminum casing with EPDM rubber and cross-linked polyethylene foam inner layers to absorb drop-test shock, with the rigging tied into the rubber layer
  • Paired the sensor with a twin-cable winch that deploys to 9 ft from the belly, keeping the sensor’s CG in line with the aircraft’s CG

Verification & Validation

Each fin prototype was held by a servo at a 5° initial deflection in the tunnel and then released. Stability was measured from the response’s settling time and RMS angular deviation. The cruciform layout settled fastest (5.96 s) with the lowest RMS deviation (0.717°), so it was selected. The container drop test and a winch-and-sensor prototype flight are gating tests: neither system goes on the competition airframe until it passes.

Failure Analysis & Learnings

The dihedral fin layout, which looked reasonable on paper, overshot nearly 3° and kept oscillating for about 6 seconds after release. A towed body behaves very differently from a free-flying one, which is why the fin choice came from measured tunnel data rather than intuition.

PRJ — SUMMER PLANE

Summer plane initiative: carbon-fiber fuselage aircraft

Initiative Lead — Summer 2026
+

Problem & Constraints

Competition season leaves no room to learn a new manufacturing process from scratch. To move the team off balsa-and-foam onto a carbon-fiber composite fuselage, I started an off-season summer plane: a full aircraft designed, built, and flown between competition cycles, so the mold-making and layup process, tooling, and quality-control method could be proven before the 2026–27 build began. We did it with a volunteer team, no prior institutional composites experience, and a single summer.

My Exact Role

I started and led the initiative. I defined the layup process, ran the build, and trained the students who will carry it forward after I graduate.

Technical Execution

  • Designed and 3D-printed segmented fuselage and nose-cone molds, and laser-cut plywood formers and bulkheads for the tooling
  • Developed the mold-prep and layup process for the carbon-fiber fuselage shells and nose sections
  • Ran coupon testing to characterize layup strength before committing to full-size parts
  • Assembled the full aircraft, with motor, propeller, and landing gear, and took it to flight test
  • Documented the process in a build guide so the initiative outlives my time on the team

Verification & Validation

Ran tensile and flexure coupon tests to check actual layup strength against datasheet fiber properties before building full-size parts, then validated the finished airframe in a successful test flight. The process now feeds directly into the 2026–27 competition aircraft’s carbon-fiber fuselage.

Failure Analysis & Learnings

The first coupons showed lower-than-expected strength from resin-rich layup and trapped air. Adjusting vacuum-bagging pressure and cure time fixed it; I learned to validate a new process on small coupons before ever cutting full-size tooling.

PRJ — WINGLET CFD

CFD study on winglets & full aircraft

Aerodynamics & Payload Lead
+

Problem & Constraints

XFLR5’s panel method couldn’t capture induced-drag reduction from winglet geometry or interference drag at the wing-fuselage junction, but the team needed a defensible drag number before committing tooling.

My Exact Role

As Aerodynamics & Payload Lead, I set up and ran the CFD studies independently, then reconciled the results against our XFLR5 model for the design review.

Technical Execution

  • Modeled several winglet geometries to compare induced-drag reduction
  • Ran full-aircraft CFD to check for interference drag at the wing-fuselage junction
  • Cross-checked CFD lift and drag results against XFLR5 to flag where panel-method assumptions broke down
  • Ran 2D/3D CFD sweeps (k-ω SST) in ANSYS Fluent and XFLR5 across Re ≈ 200k–400k, raising maximum wing lift coefficient (CL,max) by 15%

Verification & Validation

Cross-checked CFD lift and drag coefficients against the XFLR5 polars at the same operating points, and flagged the angle-of-attack range where the two methods diverged.

Failure Analysis & Learnings

The first mesh was too coarse near the wingtip and under-predicted the winglet’s drag benefit. Refining the boundary-layer mesh changed the answer enough that I built a mesh-convergence check into every CFD run afterward.

My Favorite Aircraft

10 / 10

Six vehicles that inspired how I think about design trades and innovation.

Space Shuttle Orbiter
Spacecraft

Why it inspires me: One airframe had to fly two completely different missions, a spacecraft on the way up and an unpowered glider on the way down, with zero margin to redo the landing.

Design constraint it solved: Surviving reentry heating without power, using a reusable silica-tile thermal protection system instead of a one-time ablative coating.

Lockheed SR-71 Blackbird
Reconnaissance aircraft

Why it inspires me: A jet that leaked fuel on the ground on purpose and only sealed itself once heat expanded the airframe in flight: every subsystem was designed to accept its environment instead of fighting it.

Design constraint it solved: Thermal expansion at sustained Mach 3+ cruise, solved with corrugated titanium skin panels that could expand inches without buckling.

Concorde
Supersonic airliner

Why it inspires me: A commercial aircraft that treated the low-speed/high-speed handling conflict as a shape problem to design away, not a power problem to fly through.

Design constraint it solved: Stable handling across both takeoff speeds and supersonic cruise in one fixed wing, solved with the ogee delta planform generating controlled vortex lift at low speed with no separate flaps or slats.

F-16 Fighting Falcon
Fighter jet

Why it inspires me: The first fighter designed to be aerodynamically unstable on purpose: betting the whole program on a flight computer flying what a pilot physically couldn’t.

Design constraint it solved: The maneuverability-vs-stability trade, solved with relaxed static stability plus quadruple-redundant fly-by-wire, moving the stability margin from the airframe into the control law.

SpaceX Falcon 9
Orbital-class rocket

Why it inspires me: A booster that flies its hardest, most precision-critical maneuver after its primary job is already done, purely so it can fly again.

Design constraint it solved: Reusing an orbital-class first stage, solved with propulsive landing: grid fins for aerodynamic control at reentry velocity and deep-throttling engines that can hover the stage down to near-zero velocity.

North American X-15
Hypersonic research aircraft

Why it inspires me: A research vehicle built with the explicit expectation that it would fly past the edge of what existing materials and data could predict, and still bring the pilot home.

Design constraint it solved: Aerodynamic heating and attitude control at the edge of the atmosphere, solved with an Inconel X nickel-alloy skin and reaction control thrusters for the altitudes where control surfaces had no air to bite into.
